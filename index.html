Before you begin

What assimilation is

Assimilation is a modification of a consonant under the influence of a neighbouring consonant, which makes the two sounds more alike and the articulation more economical. In Modern English it is mainly consonants that are assimilated, and the most striking changes happen when two consonants are next to each other.

Assimilation is classified by the articulatory feature that changes. This page covers the first three types. Under the title of every rule you will see three labels: its direction, its degree and its status. Learn to read them, because theory questions are asked in exactly these terms.

The three types on this page

Type 1. Point of articulation. The consonant keeps its active organ and only moves the place where the obstruction is made.

Type 2. Point of articulation and active speech organ. Both features change: the consonant is made at a new place and by a different organ.

Type 3. Manner of the production of noise. The place stays the same, but the plosive is released in a different way or not released at all.

Type 1 of 3

Assimilation affecting the point of articulation

The consonant keeps its active organ but moves its point of articulation towards the point of the neighbouring consonant.

1

Alveolar [t, d, n, l, s, z] before [θ, ð]

Regressive · partial · obligatory

The alveolar consonants [t, d, n, l, s, z] become dental when they are immediately followed by the interdental [θ] or [ð]. The tip of the tongue touches the upper teeth instead of the alveolar ridge.

Within a word

eighth [eɪt̪θ]breadth [bred̪θ]tenth [ten̪θ]health [hel̪θ]wealth [wel̪θ]

At word boundaries

at the desk [ət̪ ðə ˈdesk]read this [ˈriːd̪ ðɪs]in the room [ɪn̪ ðə ˈruːm]all the time [ˈɔːl̪ ðə ˈtaɪm]is this [ɪz̪ ðɪs]
2

Alveolar [t, d] before [r]

Regressive · partial · obligatory

The plosives [t] and [d] become post-alveolar before the post-alveolar sonorant [r]. The tip of the tongue touches the back part of the alveolar ridge.

Within a word

try [traɪ]true [truː]trip [trɪp]dream [driːm]drink [drɪŋk]dry [draɪ]

At word boundaries

at rest [ət ˈrest]would read [wʊd ˈriːd]that road [ðæt ˈrəʊd]
3

Bilabial [m] before [f, v]

Regressive · partial · obligatory

The bilabial nasal [m] becomes labio-dental before the labio-dental [f] or [v]. The lower lip is still the active organ, but it now touches the upper teeth instead of the upper lip.

Within a word

comfort [ˈkʌɱfət]triumph [ˈtraɪəɱf]nymph [nɪɱf]

At word boundaries

come for me [ˈkʌɱ fə mi]some fun [səɱ ˈfʌn]

Watch out

Do not replace [θ, ð] with [t, d, s, z]. The usual mistake is saying at the as [ət də]. The dental allophone of [t] is there to make the transition to [ð] easier, so the assimilation helps you.

Remember that the dental consonant here is still the same phoneme, only a subsidiary allophone. In Russian [т, д, н] are dental everywhere, so the English norm is the alveolar articulation and the dental one is a positional variant.

Type 2 of 3

Assimilation affecting a) the point of articulation and b) the active speech organ

Both features change at once. The consonant moves to a new point of articulation and is made by a different active organ, that is by the lower lip or by the front or the back of the tongue instead of its tip.

1

Alveolar [n] before [f, v]

Regressive · partial · obligatory

The alveolar nasal [n] becomes labio-dental before [f] or [v]. The active organ changes from the tip of the tongue to the lower lip, and the point of articulation from the alveolar ridge to the upper teeth.

Within a word

infant [ˈɪɱfənt]invite [ɪɱˈvaɪt]information [ˌɪɱfəˈmeɪʃn̩]

At word boundaries

ten forks [ˈteɱ ˈfɔːks]in front [ɪɱ ˈfrʌnt]on view [ɒɱ ˈvjuː]
2

[t, d] + [j] merge into [tʃ, dʒ]

Reciprocal · complete

The alveolar [t] or [d] and the palatal [j] influence each other and merge into the affricates [tʃ] and [dʒ]. Neither of the two sounds survives, and a third sound appears. Within words this change is historical and fixed. At word boundaries it is typical of fluent colloquial speech.

Within a word (historical)

nature [ˈneɪtʃə]picture [ˈpɪktʃə]question [ˈkwestʃən]soldier [ˈsəʊldʒə]procedure [prəˈsiːdʒə]

At word boundaries (colloquial)

what you want [ˈwɒtʃu ˈwɒnt]did you [ˈdɪdʒu]would you [ˈwʊdʒu]last year [ˈlɑːstʃɪə]
3

[s, z] before [ʃ, j]

Regressive · complete or intermediate · non-obligatory

In fluent speech the alveolar [s] and [z] become palato-alveolar [ʃ] and [ʒ] before [ʃ] or [j]. The front of the tongue, not its tip, becomes the active organ.

Within a word

horseshoe [ˈhɔːʃʃuː]

At word boundaries

this shop [ðɪʃ ˈʃɒp]this year [ðɪʃ ˈjɪə]as you like [əʒ ju ˈlaɪk]these shoes [ðiːʒ ˈʃuːz]
4

Word-final [t, d, n] before bilabials and velars

Regressive · complete or intermediate · non-obligatory

In rapid colloquial speech the word-final alveolars take the point of articulation of the next word-initial consonant. Before [p, b, m] they become bilabial, so the lips replace the tip of the tongue. Before [k, g] they become velar, so the back of the tongue replaces its tip. Careful speakers avoid these forms, but you must be able to recognise them when you listen.

Before bilabials

ten minutes [ˈtem ˈmɪnɪts]good boy [ˈɡʊb ˈbɔɪ]that man [ˈðæp ˈmæn]in bed [ɪm ˈbed]

Before velars

ten girls [ˈteŋ ˈɡɜːlz]that cup [ˈðæk ˈkʌp]good girl [ˈɡʊɡ ˈɡɜːl]in case [ɪŋ ˈkeɪs]

Watch out

Only alveolar consonants regularly assimilate in place at word boundaries. Bilabials and velars stay as they are: black box never becomes [blæp bɒks].

Rule 3 works in one direction only. [s] becomes [ʃ] before [ʃ], but [ʃ] does not become [s] before [s]: fresh sausage keeps [ʃ s].

In [tj, dj] make a clear choice: either the careful [ˈtjuːn] or the coalesced [ˈtʃuːn], never a soft Russian [т'].

Type 3 of 3

Assimilation affecting the manner of the production of noise

The point of articulation stays the same, but the way the obstruction is released changes under the influence of the following consonant. English plosives do not always have a sudden oral release.

1

Loss of plosion

Regressive · partial · obligatory

When a plosive is followed by another plosive or an affricate, the first plosive has no audible release. If both consonants have the same point of articulation, the organs of speech stay in one position and the result sounds like one plosive with a long hold. If the points differ, the closure for the second consonant is made before the first one is released.

Same point of articulation

bookcase [ˈbʊkkeɪs]what time [ˈwɒt ˈtaɪm]went down [ˈwent ˈdaʊn]that child [ˈðæt ˈtʃaɪld]big cat [ˈbɪɡ ˈkæt]

Different points of articulation

act [ækt]talked [tɔːkt]object [ˈɒbdʒɪkt]lecture [ˈlektʃə]good book [ˈɡʊd ˈbʊk]hot bottle [ˈhɒt ˈbɒtl̩]
2

Nasal plosion

Regressive · partial · obligatory

When a plosive is followed by the nasal [n] or [m], the oral closure is kept. The compressed air escapes through the nose, because the soft palate is lowered. No vowel is heard between the plosive and the nasal.

Within a word

button [ˈbʌtn̩]sudden [ˈsʌdn̩]written [ˈrɪtn̩]happen [ˈhæpn̩]didn't [ˈdɪdn̩t]submarine [ˌsʌbməˈriːn]

At word boundaries

stop moaning [ˈstɒp ˈməʊnɪŋ]not now [ˈnɒt ˈnaʊ]sub man [ˈsʌb ˈmæn]
3

Lateral plosion

Regressive · partial · obligatory

When a plosive is followed by [l], the closure is not released until [l] is formed. The air escapes suddenly over the sides of the tongue, which are lowered, while the tip stays on the alveolar ridge.

Within a word

little [ˈlɪtl̩]middle [ˈmɪdl̩]cattle [ˈkætl̩]candle [ˈkændl̩]please [pliːz]black [blæk]

At word boundaries

at last [ət ˈlɑːst]bad luck [ˈbæd ˈlʌk]

Typical mistakes

A vowel inserted before [n] or [l]. Do not say [ˈbʌtən] or [ˈlɪtəl]. Keep the tongue on the alveolar ridge and let the air out through the nose or over the sides of the tongue. The result is a syllabic [n̩] or [l̩].

Two explosions in a plosive cluster. Pronouncing both [k] and [t] in act with a full release does not change the meaning, but it sounds un-English.

1

Name the type

Which type of assimilation is at work in each word or phrase?

1. eighth [eɪt̪θ]

Correct. Type 1. The alveolar [t] becomes dental before [θ]. The tip of the tongue is still the active organ, only the point of articulation moves.

2. infant [ˈɪɱfənt]

Correct. Type 2. [n] becomes labio-dental before [f]: the lower lip replaces the tip of the tongue, so the active organ changes too.

3. button [ˈbʌtn̩]

Correct. Type 3, nasal plosion. The point of articulation of [t] does not change, but the air escapes through the nose and the [n] is syllabic.

4. dry [draɪ]

Correct. Type 1. [d] becomes post-alveolar before [r]: the tip of the tongue moves back, but it is still the active organ.

5. did you [ˈdɪdʒu]

Correct. Type 2, rule 2. [d] and [j] merge into the affricate [dʒ]. This is reciprocal and complete assimilation.

6. act [ækt]

Correct. Type 3, loss of plosion. The closure for [t] is made before [k] is released, so the first plosive is inaudible.

7. some fun [səɱ ˈfʌn]

Correct. Type 1, rule 3. [m] becomes labio-dental, but the lower lip was the active organ before the change as well, so only the point of articulation is affected. Compare infant, where [n] is made by the tongue and the organ changes.

8. this year [ðɪʃ ˈjɪə]

Correct. Type 2, rule 3. [s] becomes palato-alveolar [ʃ] and the front of the tongue becomes the active organ. The assimilation is non-obligatory.

9. at last [ət ˈlɑːst]

Correct. Type 3, lateral plosion. The tip of the tongue stays on the alveolar ridge and the air escapes over the lowered sides of the tongue.

2

What does the consonant become?

Choose the new point of articulation of the consonant in bold.

1. The [t] in tenth becomes

Correct. Dental. Before [θ] the tip of the tongue touches the upper teeth: [ten̪θ].

2. The [d] in dream becomes

Correct. Post-alveolar. Before [r] the tip of the tongue moves to the back part of the alveolar ridge.

3. The [m] in triumph becomes

Correct. Labio-dental. The lower lip touches the upper teeth instead of the upper lip: [ˈtraɪəɱf]. This is Type 1, because the active organ stays the same.

4. The [n] in on view becomes

Correct. Labio-dental [ɒɱ ˈvjuː]. Here the active organ changes as well, from the tip of the tongue to the lower lip, so this is Type 2.

5. The [s] in this shop becomes

Correct. Palato-alveolar [ʃ], made by the front of the tongue: [ðɪʃ ˈʃɒp].

6. In rapid speech the [d] in good boy becomes

Correct. Bilabial [ˈɡʊb ˈbɔɪ]. The lips replace the tip of the tongue. The assimilation is non-obligatory, so careful speech keeps [d].

7. In rapid speech the [n] in in case becomes

Correct. Velar [ɪŋ ˈkeɪs]. The back of the tongue replaces its tip before [k].

3

True or false

Decide whether each statement about the three types is right.

1. In eighth the [t] is pronounced with the tip of the tongue against the upper teeth.

Correct. True. This is the dental allophone of [t], used before [θ, ð].

2. The assimilation of alveolar [t, d, n, l, s, z] before [θ, ð] is non-obligatory.

Correct. False. It is obligatory: it belongs to the norm and is used in all styles of speech.

3. Before [r] the plosives [t, d] become dental.

Correct. False. They become post-alveolar. The tongue moves back, not forward: try, dream.

4. In infant the active organ changes from the tip of the tongue to the lower lip.

Correct. True. That is exactly why this rule belongs to Type 2 and not to Type 1.

5. In what you want the sounds [t] and [j] merge into [dʒ].

Correct. False. The voiceless [t] gives the voiceless affricate [tʃ]: [ˈwɒtʃu ˈwɒnt]. [dʒ] comes from [d], as in did you.

6. A careful speaker keeps the alveolar [n] in ten minutes.

Correct. True. The bilabial [ˈtem ˈmɪnɪts] is non-obligatory and belongs to rapid colloquial speech, but you should recognise it when you listen.

7. In little the air escapes over the lowered sides of the tongue.

Correct. True. This is lateral plosion, and the result is a syllabic [l̩]: [ˈlɪtl̩].

8. In Type 3 the point of articulation of the first plosive changes.

Correct. False. In Type 3 the point of articulation stays the same. What changes is the manner of the release.

Correct. [ˈneɪtʃə]. Inside the word the coalescence of [t] and [j] is historical and fixed in the standard pronunciation.
